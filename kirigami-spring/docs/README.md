## What it is

One cut, and a disc of card becomes a spring. The cut is a spiral that starts near the centre and
winds outward without reaching the edge. The paper between neighbouring turns of the cut is a
single spiral strip: its inner end holds an uncut hub, its outer end an uncut rim. Hold the rim
down and lift the hub, and the strip climbs into a conical helix. Let go, and it settles back toward
the flat sheet.

## Geometry

The cut is an Archimedean spiral, the curve whose radius grows in proportion to the angle:

    r(θ) = r_hub + arms · b · θ / 2π,   θ from 0 to turns · 2π

Neighbouring turns of the cut are `arms · b` apart, so with one arm the strip has the constant width
`b` all along, and with two arms the two cuts, half a turn apart, leave two interleaved strips of
width `b`. Two arms hold the hub from opposite sides, so it rises level instead of tilting.

The generator sizes the rest around the strip: the hub radius is `max(2b, 0.12 R)` and the rim is
`max(2b, 8 mm)` wide, where `R` is the disc radius. It refuses a spiral that would run into the rim.
The cut is drawn as a polyline with a vertex every 6° of turn. `pliego.papercut.analyze` then checks
that the disc is one piece and that every strip is at least the stock's minimum feature wide; the
narrowest bridge it reports is the strip width, a few hundredths of a millimetre less where the
polyline cuts the chord.

## The mechanism

The document is physical (tier C). Eight pins hold the rim at points around its middle circle, and
three points of the hub are pinned to a `translate` driver that lifts them straight up. The **Lift
the centre** control drives it, up to the disc's radius. That range is an estimate. How high card
can rise before it creases or tears depends on the stock, and the engine decides.

The studio preview keeps the sheet flat and disables the Lift control. Version 1 frames cannot
separate the two lips of a cut, and the rising strip bends and twists continuously, which a rigid
model of this sheet cannot show. The extension needs the physics engine.

## Making one by hand

1. Print the PDF on cardstock, or draw a spiral on a disc: mark the centre, then sketch a curve
   that moves outward by one strip width per turn.
2. Pierce the cut's inner end with a craft knife and cut along the line, on a cutting mat. Stop at
   its outer end, leaving the rim whole.
3. Hold the rim flat on the table, pinch the hub and lift.

For two arms, cut both spirals, each starting on opposite sides of the hub. A plotter, a craft
cutter or a laser cuts the pattern from the SVG, DXF or HPGL export. Polypropylene makes a spring
that survives repeated stretching.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `diameter` | disc diameter, mm |
| `turns` | turns of each spiral cut |
| `width` | strip width `b`, mm (the distance between neighbouring turns of the strip) |
| `arms` | one spiral cut, or two interleaved cuts |
| `stock` | cardstock, copy paper, kraft or polypropylene |

## History and sources

The spiral that grows by equal steps per turn is named after Archimedes, who studied it in his
treatise *On Spirals*. This cartridge makes no historical claim about spiral paper cuts as a craft.

- T. L. Heath (ed.), *The Works of Archimedes*, Cambridge University Press (1897): *On Spirals*.
