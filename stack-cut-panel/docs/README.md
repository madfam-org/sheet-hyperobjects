## What it is

Instead of folding the paper and cutting once through the folds, cut once through a **stack**:
every sheet in it gets exactly the same openings. The sources cited below report that papel
picado, the Mexican tissue-paper papercut credited to **San Salvador Huixcolotla, Puebla**, is
cut this way, with chisels (*fierritos*) and a mallet, through stacks of about 40 to 50 sheets at a
time [1]. This cartridge models a small stack of tissue panels cut once, flat, coming off the stack
in a run of colours.

The credit for the tradition belongs to the papel picado artisans of San Salvador Huixcolotla. The
motifs here are **original geometric designs** generated by `main.py` in the same idiom as the
commons' `papel-picado-banner`: a rosette of petals with a ring of diamonds and small flowers in the
corners, and a sun of rays in a lattice of diamonds. No artisan's design is copied. The object is
offered as a way to understand and make a stack-cut panel, not as a substitute for the artisans'
work.

## Geometry

Each panel is a tissue rectangle with a zigzag bottom edge, a solid frame and an uncut **string
strip** at the top. The openings are drawn whole, directly on the flat panel: a stack is cut flat,
not folded, so there is no fold to mirror them, and the motifs are symmetric by construction.

- **Rosette.** Twelve petals around a round centre, a ring of twelve diamonds outside it, and a
  four-petal flower in each corner.
- **Lattice.** A round centre with sixteen rays, long and short in turn, inside a staggered lattice
  of diamonds kept clear of the sun.

`pliego.papercut.analyze` checks one panel, since all are identical: one connected piece and every
bridge at least 1.5 mm, the `papel-de-china` stock card's minimum feature. The panels are separate
sheets laid 0.03 mm apart in the stack, without joins: a cut stack is loose.

## The fold program

The cut itself is not animated. Version 1 documents describe the sheets after cutting, so the first
step only narrates it: the whole stack is cut at once, flat. The second step folds every panel's
string strip over, as each is hung on a string. The program is physical, and the studio preview
plays it. To string the panels into a banner that moves in the wind, see `papel-picado-banner`.

## Making one by hand

- **With a knife.** Stack a few sheets of tissue on a cutting mat, fix the corners with masking
  tape, lay the printed pattern on top and cut through all layers with a sharp craft knife. Tissue
  is fragile, so keep the blade sharp and the stack small.
- **By machine.** Export SVG, DXF or HPGL and cut one sheet at a time on a plotter or craft cutter
  with a sharp blade and low pressure on a tacky mat. A laser cutter vaporises tissue at very low
  power: test first, and never leave it unattended.
- **Hanging.** Lay a string along the top strip of each panel, fold the strip over it and glue it.

## History and sources

The sources below credit papel picado to San Salvador Huixcolotla, Puebla, and report that:

- it is cut from tissue paper (*papel de china*) with chisels (*fierritos*) and a mallet, through
  stacks of about 40 to 50 sheets at a time [1];
- it adorns Día de Muertos ofrendas, fiestas and weddings [1];
- the papel picado of Huixcolotla was declared cultural heritage of the State of Puebla in 1998 [2].

This cartridge makes no claim beyond these sources.

1. Wikipedia, "Papel picado": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papel_picado
2. México Desconocido, article on the papel picado of Huixcolotla:
