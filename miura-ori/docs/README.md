## Geometry

The sheet is an `m × n` grid of congruent parallelograms with sides `a` (along the straight row
lines) and `b` (along the zigzag columns) and acute sector angle `γ`. Every interior vertex has
degree four. The row lines alternate mountain and valley from one panel to the next; each zigzag
column is uniformly mountain or valley, alternating from column to column. The pattern is generated
by `pliego.tessellate.miura` from its own coordinates; no published crease pattern is copied.

## The angle law (one degree of freedom)

The whole motion is described by one number, `η ∈ [0, γ]`: each straight row line folds into a
planar zigzag whose segments turn by `±η`. With `cx = cos γ / cos η` and `cz² = 1 − cx²` the
folded vertices are exactly

    X(i, j) = (i·a·cos η + (j mod 2)·b·cx,  (i mod 2)·a·sin η,  j·b·cz)

and every panel stays a congruent, flat parallelogram — the motion is rigid. The fold angles are

    cos ρ_row = (cz² − cx²·sin²η) / sin²γ
    cos ρ_col = (cz²·cos 2η − cx²·sin²η) / sin²γ

`η = 0` is the flat sheet and `η = γ` is the flat-folded stack (both angles 180°).

Schenk and Guest describe the same family by the angle `θ` between a facet and the reference plane;
the two parameters are related by `sin η = sin θ · sin γ`. In those terms the **in-plane Poisson
ratio** is

    ν = −cos²θ · tan²γ

(strain along the rows over strain along the zigzag, with a minus sign). It is negative: pulling
the sheet open in one direction opens it in the other as well, which is why a folded Miura sheet
packs and deploys in a single motion. For example `(γ, θ) = (60°, 60°)` gives `ν = −0.75` and
`(45°, 45°)` gives `ν = −0.50`.

## What the engine shows

The document's fold program is **kinematic** (rigid origami): two steps drive every crease to the
exact rigid state at `η = fold·γ/2` and at `η = fold·γ`, and each step's note prints the values
of `η`, `θ`, `ρ_row`, `ρ_col` and `ν` at that state. Scrub the *Fold* control to move along the
one-parameter family. The document also carries a `target:half-folded` frame whose vertices are the
closed form above at `η = γ/2`; the commons evidence checks that its fold angles match the angle law
to within 1e-5° and that a finite-difference Poisson ratio of the closed-form vertices matches
`−cos²θ·tan²γ`.

## Making it

Print or plot the crease pattern (SVG, PDF, DXF or HPGL export) and score every line before folding
— kami or 80 g/m² copy paper takes a bone-folder score well; for tant, score with a plotter blade at
reduced pressure. Pre-fold all zigzag columns as accordion pleats, then collapse the row lines one
row at a time. Once every crease is set, the sheet opens and closes by pulling two opposite corners.

## History and sources

The fold is named after the astrophysicist Koryo Miura, who proposed it for packaging and deploying
large membranes in space.

- K. Miura, *Method of packaging and deployment of large membranes in space*, The Institute of Space
  and Astronautical Science Report 618 (1985).
- M. Schenk and S. D. Guest, "Geometry of Miura-folded metamaterials", *PNAS* 110 (9), 3276–3281
  (2013) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3587190 (the Poisson ratio above).
- Wikipedia, "Miura fold" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miura_fold
