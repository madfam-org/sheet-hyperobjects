## What it is

Cut one outline from a sheet of card, score twelve lines, and fold: the walls rise around the base,
the flaps slip inside, and the lid closes over them. The cube holds itself shut by its tucked
flaps, with no glue. It is the cut-and-fold idea of kirigami applied to the simplest closed solid,
and the same tuck-flap principle that closes many folding cartons.

## Geometry

The net is the 1-4-1 cross. A row of four squares of side `s` wraps the cube from bottom to front:
**base**, **back wall**, **lid** and **front wall**. Two **side walls** are hinged to the back
wall, one on each side. Every face-to-face hinge is a score folded to 90°.

A cube has twelve edges. Five are hinges of the net, so seven are seams that the folding has to
close, and each seam gets one flap:

- two flaps on the base and two on the lid fold inside the side walls;
- two flaps on the front wall fold inside the side walls;
- the **tuck flap** on the front wall's free edge slides in along the base.

The flaps are trapezoids with 45° ends. Each starts 1 mm in from the corners, so no two flaps share
a vertex and none touches a neighbouring wall in the flat net. Their depth is the `flap` fraction
of the side; the cartridge refuses flaps that leave less than 5 mm of straight edge.

Before writing the document, the script poses the closed cube with
`pliego.kinematics.pose_arrangement` and measures the six seam corners it has to bring together:
the front wall's free corners onto the base, and the side walls' free corners onto the lid and
the base. At the defaults they meet within 10⁻¹³ mm. The closed state is written as the
`target:closed` frame.

## The fold program

The net is a tree of faces with no interior vertex, so every fold angle is free and the motion is
exact rigid kinematics. The program is in `kinematic` mode:

1. fold the two base flaps up;
2. raise the back wall, and swing the side walls in beside the base;
3. fold in the flaps of the lid and the front, and the tuck flap;
4. close the lid and slide the tuck flap in along the base.

Flaps that lie against a wall stop at 88° instead of 90°, so the preview shows them just inside
the wall rather than in its plane (real flaps sit one paper thickness inside). The base is the
root of the studio's face tree and stays on the table. The program ends in the closed cube.

## Making one by hand

1. Print the PDF on cardstock: cut lines, and the scores drawn as valley folds.
2. Score every fold line with a bone folder or a blunt stylus against a ruler, then cut out the
   outline.
3. Fold every score to 90°, inward.
4. Fold the base flaps up, raise the walls, fold in the remaining flaps and close the lid, sliding
   the tuck flap in along the base.

A plotter or craft cutter can score and cut in one job from the SVG, DXF or HPGL export (score
pass first). Heavier card holds the tuck more firmly.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `side` | cube side `s`, mm |
| `flap` | depth of every flap and of the tuck flap, as a fraction of the side |
| `stock` | cardstock 250, 300 or 160 g/m², or kraft 120 g/m² |

## History and sources

Unfolding a polyhedron into a single connected net, and folding it back, is a classical topic of
geometric folding. This cartridge makes no historical claim about tuck-closed cubes as a craft.

- E. D. Demaine and J. O'Rourke, *Geometric Folding Algorithms: Linkages, Origami, Polyhedra*,
  Cambridge University Press (2007): part III, polyhedra, nets and unfoldings.
