## What it is

A tunnel book is a row of cut-paper frames standing one behind the other. You look through the
window of the front cover and the scenes of the frames behind it overlap into a small stage with
real depth. The sides fold like an accordion, so the book closes flat and opens when the cover is
pulled away from the back.

This cartridge makes the whole book from **one sheet** and one kind of glue joint:

- `layers` frames (3 to 6, both covers counted): a front cover with a window, middle frames with
  cut scenes and a solid back scene;
- `scene`: four original papercut landscapes generated by `main.py` from simple shapes —
  `forest` (fir trees on rolling ground, a hanging canopy on the cover), `sea` (waves, a sailing
  boat on a middle layer, clouds on the cover), `city` (a skyline of flat, gabled, stepped and
  antenna roofs) and `mountains` (ridges of peaks behind foreground firs);
- size: `frame_width`, `frame_height`, the open `depth` between layers and the card, with the
  presets `pocket` (100 × 70 mm), `postcard` (148 × 105 mm) and `large` (200 × 140 mm, five
  layers).

## Geometry

The frames sit in one column of the sheet, the back scene at the bottom and the cover at the top.
With frame width W, frame height H, open spacing s and the display angle φ = 30°, each gap between
two frames uses a length D = s / cos φ of wall and strap (D = 40.4 mm for the postcard preset).

| part | where | folds | edge name |
|---|---|---|---|
| strap | a band B = W/4 (16–40 mm) wide at the centre of a frame's top or bottom edge, alternately, length D | hinge 90° + φ at each end, peak 2φ in the middle | `strap-fold`, `strap-peak` |
| side wall | on both side edges of every frame except the back, length D | hinge 90° + φ, peak 2φ | `wall-fold`, `wall-peak` |
| glue tab | at the far end of each wall, 8–14 mm wide, tapered | 90° + φ | `glue-tab` |
| window | every frame except the back, inside side margins of at least the tab width + 3 mm and top/bottom margins of at least D/4 + 3 mm | cut | — |

The cover's free edge is named `cover-edge` (you pull it) and the back scene's side edges
`back-zone` (the last two tabs land beside them).

**Why it is one sheet.** A frame can only be hinged to paper along its edges, and a wall that is
hinged to the frame in front of it cannot also be hinged to the frame behind it in a flat sheet.
The straps carry the chain from frame to frame, so every wall is hinged to the frame in front and
glued, by its tab, to the side margin of the frame behind (on the face that looks toward the
cover, hidden by the margin of the frame in front). The glue closes every loop, and it is the only
glue in the book.

**One angle drives everything.** Walls and straps fold inward with the same angle φ: hinge
90° + φ, peak 2φ (opposite sense), tab 90° + φ. Every crease angle is affine in φ, so every
state between closed and open is an exact rigid state. Because each strap turns the chain through
a U, consecutive frames show opposite faces of the sheet: the step note and the read-proof line
say which frames face you with the front of the sheet, so you know where to draw or print.

**The scenes.** Each windowed frame's window is one polygon: the window rectangle whose bottom
edge is replaced by a skyline (kept material below it) and, on some covers, whose top edge is
replaced by a hanging canopy or clouds. Every silhouette grows from the frame's margin, so the
kept material is one piece. Deeper layers sit higher, so they show above the layers in front.
Tree tiers are dropped when a notch would be narrower than 1.5 × the stock's minimum feature,
trunks, masts and antennas are at least 1.6 × the minimum feature wide, and waves and peaks are
kept no steeper than about 1.5 so that no needle forms.

**Self-checks (fail loud).** `main.py` builds every windowed frame on its own and runs
`pliego.papercut.analyze` with the stock's minimum feature (1.5 mm for 160 g/m² card, 2 mm for
250 and 300 g/m²): one piece of kept material and no bridge thinner than the minimum. It poses
the sheet at five states from closed to open and checks that every glue tab lies on its zone
(about 2e-6 mm), that every frame is parallel to the back, that the frames stand 3 mm apart
closed and s apart open, and that the cover stands square in front of the back.

## The fold program

The initial frame is the **closed book**: the glue is set and the frames stand 3 mm apart (about
the thickness of the folded walls), φ ≈ 85.7° for the postcard preset. The program has one
kinematic step, **Pull the cover: the tunnel opens**, which drives every wall, strap and tab
crease to its open angle (φ = 30°: hinges 120°, peaks 60°). It is exact at every instant.

**Preview and engine.** The studio preview runs the whole opening (sequence control `Open the
tunnel`): the back scene stays put, the walls and straps unfold and the cover comes toward you.
Nothing needs the physics engine to be shown. The preview does not draw paper thickness, does not
show the glue (the tabs simply stay on their zones because the angles agree), and draws no art on
the faces; the physics engine will add card stiffness, thickness and friction.

## Making one by hand

1. Export the net (`svg`, `dxf`, `pdf` or `hpgl`): outline and windows on the cut layer, the
   folds on the score layers (mountain and valley as written in the document). The net is
   W + 2(D + tab) wide; the read-proof line prints its size (253 × 541 mm for the postcard
   preset, so a 305 × 610 mm cutting mat or two sheets joined at a strap).
2. Score every fold, then cut the outline and the windows. Draw or paint the scenes first if you
   like, each frame on the side the step note names.
3. Fold every strap and wall: hinges and tabs one way, peaks the other, as scored.
4. Close the book from the back: lay each frame on the one behind it, and glue each wall's tab to
   the side margin of the frame behind, on the face that looks toward the cover.
5. When the glue is dry, hold the back scene and pull the cover. The tunnel stands open with its
   walls in a shallow V.

## Parameters

| `scene` | `forest`, `sea`, `city` or `mountains` | `forest` | |

| `frame_height` | height of every frame | 105 mm | 60–180 mm |

`large` (200 × 140 mm, 45 mm, 5 layers).

## History and sources

Paper peepshows — cut-paper panels set one behind the other and seen through a peephole, joined by
folding sides like an accordion — were made in the eighteenth century by the Augsburg engraver and
print-seller Martin Engelbrecht (1684–1756); the Cooper-Hewitt design museum library acquired an
eighteenth-century tunnel book attributed to him (Smithsonian Libraries, «Martin Engelbrecht»,
https://library.si.edu/es/tags/martin-engelbrecht). In the nineteenth century they were sold as
souvenirs; the Victoria and Albert Museum holds «The Tunnel», a hand-coloured etched paper
peepshow published about 1825 with five cut-out panels and a peephole that expands to about
62 cm, and notes that the Thames Tunnel opened on 25 March 1843 and that its association with
these devices survives in the name «tunnel book», most used in the United States (V&A,
«The Tunnel», https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1418244/the-tunnel-paper-peepshow).
Today the tunnel book is taught in libraries with M-shaped accordion strips on both sides, the
pages glued to the accordions or sandwiched between them (Topeka & Shawnee County Public Library,
«Tunnel books: an easy step by step», https://tscpl.org/articles/tunnel-books-an-easy-step-by-step).

This cartridge keeps the format — accordion sides, layered windows, a solid back — and changes the
construction: the walls and straps are part of one cut sheet, and only the wall tabs are glued.
That one-sheet construction, the fold program and all four scenes are original.
