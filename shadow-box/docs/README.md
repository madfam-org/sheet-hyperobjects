## What it is

A shadow box is a shallow box with a window, holding a scene built in layers. Here every layer is
a card frame with a papercut scene, and the frames stand one behind the other, a fixed distance
apart. Seen through the window, and better still lit from behind, the layers read as depth: a
foreground that frames the view, middle grounds, and a back panel that closes the scene.

The cartridge makes the whole object from flat card:

- **the box**, from one scored sheet;
- **the frames** (`layers`, 3 to 8), each with a scene window;
- **the spacers**, two per gap, each a strip folded into a square tube.

Four original scenes are generated by the script: `forest`, `sea`, `night-city` and `garden`.

## Geometry

**Box.** The net is a row of four panels: the base, the front (with the window), the top and the
back. Two side walls are hinged to the front panel's side edges. The base and the top each carry a
pair of side flaps that are glued inside the side walls. The back carries a pair of side flaps that
only tuck in, and a tuck flap on its free edge that slides in along the base, so the back can be
opened again. Every flap starts 1 mm in from its corner and is tapered at 45°, so no two flaps
meet. The inside depth is (`layers` + 1) × `spacing` plus the card: one gap in front of the first
frame, one between each pair of frames and one behind the last.

**Window.** Its side margins are wide enough to hide the spacers (at least the spacer band plus
3 mm) and its top and bottom margins are 10% of the height (at least 12 mm). The window must stay
at least 40 mm in each direction, or the script stops.

**Frames.** Each frame is 2 mm narrower and 1 mm lower than the inside of the box. Its scene
window lines up with the box's window. The kept material is a silhouette that stands on the
bottom margin, and on the front frame also a scalloped edge that hangs from the top margin
(leaves, clouds or a vine). With depth the ground line rises and the shapes get smaller.

- **forest:** two round trees framing the view in front, firs behind, more and smaller with depth;
- **sea:** pointed wave crests on every frame, a rock, one or two sailboats, an island with a
  lighthouse at the back;
- **night city:** blocks of buildings with flat, gabled, stepped and antenna roofs; the two far
  frames have lit windows cut in them, and the back panel is pierced with a moon and stars for a
  light behind the box;
- **garden:** tulips in front, a picket fence, clipped hedges and round trees.

Stems, trunks, masts and antennas are at least 1.6 times the stock's minimum feature. Every frame
is checked with `pliego.papercut.analyze` before the document is written: the kept material must be
one piece and its narrowest bridge must reach the minimum feature (1.5 mm for 160 g/m² card, 2 mm
for 250 and 300 g/m²). The script prints the narrowest bridge of every frame.

**Spacers.** A spacer is a strip of four panels and a tab. Folded on its three scores it becomes a
square tube whose outer side is exactly `spacing`. Its tab is glued inside the tube. One face is
glued along the side margin of a frame (`spacer_glue_zone`), and the opposite face goes on the next
layer. Two tubes fill every gap. They are rooted on frame faces: the tubes in front of each frame
on its front face, and the tubes behind the last frame on that frame's back face.

## The fold program

Every crease is a score folded to 90° and every sheet is a tree of panels, so each step is an exact
rigid motion (`kinematic` mode). Flaps that lie against a wall stop at 89°, and the spacer tabs at
88°, just inside their walls. The frames stand in their final places throughout. The program folds
the spacers and the box around them:

1. fold the spacer strips into tubes;
2. fold up the base's side flaps;
3. raise the front panel;
4. swing the side walls back over the base flaps;
5. fold the top's flaps down and the top back;
6. close the back: side flaps, tuck flap and back panel.

The assembled state is written as the `target:assembled` frame. Before it writes the document, the
script folds the box to that state and checks that its seams close: the side walls meet the base
and the top, and the back meets the base. It also checks that every folded spacer stands
`spacing` deep on the right side of its frame. **The preview** plays the whole program.

**What needs the physics engine.** The glue joints are given as interfaces (`spacer_glue_zone`,
`spacer_glue_face`) rather than as `glue` joins, because the frames stand in place before the box
is folded. A glued diorama in the engine would need the joins and an initial frame that satisfies
them. The engine would also bring the slight sag of the long top panel and the spring of the tuck
flaps.

## Making one by hand

1. Export the sheets (`svg`, `dxf`, `pdf` or `hpgl`). Cut the frames on a cutting machine, or with
   a sharp knife: the inside corners first, then the long edges.
2. Score the box and the spacer strips and cut them out. Print or paint the outside of the box on
   the back of the sheet: the scored side is the inside.
3. Fold each spacer into a tube and glue its tab inside.
4. Build the stack from the front. Glue two tubes on frame 1's side margins, the next frame on
   them, and so on; glue the last two tubes on the back of the last frame.
5. Fold the base flaps up, raise the front and glue the side walls over the base flaps. Fold the top
   back and glue its flaps inside the side walls. You now have a tray with a window.
6. Slide the stack in from the back with frame 1 against the window, and glue the front tubes to
   the front panel. Close the back and tuck its flaps in.
7. For the night city, put a small light behind the back panel.

## Parameters

| id | meaning | default | range |
|---|---|---|---|
| `scene` | `forest`, `sea`, `night-city`, `garden` | `forest` | — |
| `layers` | cut frames between the window and the back | 5 | 3–8 |
| `width`, `height` | inside of the box | 160 × 120 mm | 100–240, 80–200 mm |
| `spacing` | gap between layers, the spacer tube's side | 10 mm | 6–25 mm |
| `stock` | frame card (box 300 g/m², spacers 250 g/m²) | `cardstock-250` | 160, 250, 300 g/m² |

Presets: `small` (120 × 90 mm, four frames), `classic` (160 × 120 mm, five frames), `deep`
(210 × 150 mm, eight frames) and `night-city` (six frames).

## History and sources

Layered cut scenes viewed in a box are an old format. Around 1730 the Augsburg engraver and
publisher Martin Engelbrecht (1684–1756) issued sets of engraved and cut cards which, arranged one
behind the other, make a scene in perspective. Typically eight cards were set into a peep box, and
at least 41 sets are known (Wikipedia, «Martin Engelbrecht»,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Engelbrecht; Stephen J. Gertz, «The Miniature Theaters of
Martin Engelbrecht», Fine Books Magazine, 2009,
https://www.finebooksmagazine.com/fine-books-news/miniature-theaters-martin-engelbrecht). The same
Wikipedia article describes them as forerunners of the peepshow books published in London in the
mid-nineteenth century. The papercut light box and the layered shadow box made today belong to
the same family. No historical or commercial design is
copied here: the box, the spacers and the scenes are computed by this cartridge.
