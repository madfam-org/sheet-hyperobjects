## What it is

A toy theatre is a stage in miniature, cut from printed card: a proscenium arch at the front,
wings standing in rows at the sides, a backdrop at the back, and flats (scenery or characters)
pushed on from the wings. This cartridge builds one from flat card:

- **the stage**, a low platform scored from one sheet;
- **the proscenium**, with a curtain drawn back to tie-backs and a scalloped valance;
- **`wings` pairs of wings** (2 to 4), a **ground row** (a low scenery piece in front of the back)
  and a **backdrop**;
- **the flat**, standing on a folded foot whose handle runs out past the side of the stage.

Three original scene sets are generated by the script (`scene`): `forest` (tree wings, a ground
row of hills and firs, a cottage), `street` (house wings with windows and stepped gables, a ground
row of roofs, a coach) and `seaside` (cliff wings, a ground row of waves, a sailboat). The
backdrop is plain card, left for painting or printing.

## Geometry

**Stage.** The floor (`width` × `depth`) has a band of four skirts, each 30 mm high, hinged to its
front edge. The band wraps round under the floor's edges: front, right, back and left. Flaps on
the band's top edge are glued under the floor, and a tab at the band's end is glued inside the
front skirt. The band starts 0.5 mm in from the floor's front-left corner, which leaves that
corner to the floor alone. The floor carries one slit per tab, 1 mm longer than the tab.

**Pieces.** Every standing piece has tabs on its bottom edge (14 mm wide, 18 mm deep, tapered).
Each tab goes down through its slit and folds back flat under the floor.

- The **proscenium** is as wide as the stage and 0.72 times as high, with a round crest. Its
  opening is framed by posts (13% of the width), a header (20% of the height) and an 8 mm sill.
  The curtain is the kept material at the sides of the opening: wide under the valance, drawn
  in to a tie-back at 42% of the height, flaring again to the sill. The valance hangs in five
  scallops.
- The **wings** are a quarter of the stage width. They stand in rows behind the proscenium, the
  left and right wing of a row mirror images. Their inner edge carries the scene: a trunk and a
  crown of three round bulges, a house front with six windows and a stepped gable, or a jagged
  cliff. The wings reach just behind the header, so their tops are never seen.
- The **ground row** stands in front of the backdrop: a band of card whose top edge is the
  scene's silhouette.
- The **backdrop** is plain.

**Flat.** The flat stands on a 5 mm base strip. Its foot, folded back under the base strip, lies
on the floor, and continues into a handle 8 mm wide that runs to the right, past the side of the
stage. The flat starts hidden behind the right wing of the first row and travels along the first
passage between the wing rows to the centre of the stage. Its travel is 0.375 × `width`.

Stems, masts and other necks are at least 3.2 mm wide. Every piece and the flat pass
`pliego.papercut.analyze` (one connected piece, narrowest bridge at least 2 mm). The script
prints the narrowest bridge found.

## The fold program and the slide

The **fold program** (`kinematic`, every step an exact rigid motion of a tree of panels):

1. fold in the flaps on the band's top edge;
2. fold the front skirt down under the floor's front edge;
3. fold the right skirt back;
4. fold the back skirt across;
5. fold the left skirt, and glue the closing tab inside the front;
6. push every tab through its slit and fold it back under the floor.

The studio roots the face tree of each sheet on the face holding its lowest-left vertex. For the
stage that is the floor, so the floor stays in place while the band wraps round it. The standing
pieces stand in their final places throughout. Before it writes the document the script folds
the band and checks that it closes round the floor: each corner of the band must meet its corner
of the floor. The assembled state is the `target:assembled` frame.

The **slide** is a `translate` mechanism on the flat, a rigid (kinematic) sheet with no joins, so
the `slide-in` control (0 to the full travel, in mm) plays in the studio preview. Its foot is
folded in the initial frame and stays folded: a mechanism moves the flat as one rigid piece.

**What needs the physics engine.** Tabs held by friction in their slits, the glue under the
floor, and the slight flex of a long handle. The preview folds each tab at the floor plane and
leaves the card thickness out.

## Making one by hand

1. Export the sheets (`svg`, `dxf`, `pdf` or `hpgl`). Print or paint the scenery first, then cut.
   Cut the curtain and the windows with a sharp knife. Cut the floor's slits.
2. Score the stage, fold the band round under the floor and glue its flaps and its closing tab.
3. Score the tabs of every piece. Push the tabs through their slits from above and fold them back
   under the floor: the backdrop first, then the ground row, the wings from the back row forward,
   and the proscenium last.
4. Fold the flat's foot back. Lay the flat in the first passage behind the right wing, with its
   handle out past the stage, and push it on.

## History and sources

The toy theatre grew out of the London stage of the Regency. In 1811 the publisher William West
issued a souvenir sheet of a popular pantomime. By the middle of 1812 he and his rivals were
selling sheets of characters, scenery and wings, card prosceniums and wooden stages, sold «penny
plain, twopence coloured» (Pollock's Toy Museum, «William West and the Regency Toy Theatre»,
https://www.pollockstoymuseum.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/William-West-and-the-Regency-Toy-Theatre.pdf).
The English Juvenile Drama turned more than three hundred London plays into toy-theatre
productions in the first half of the nineteenth century. Robert Louis Stevenson praised it in his
essay «A Penny Plain and Twopence Coloured» (1884), and George Speaight wrote its history,
*Juvenile Drama: The History of the English Toy Theatre* (1946) (Wikipedia, «Toy theater»,
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toy_theater).

In German-speaking Europe the *Papiertheater* appeared around 1810. Its publishers included
Trentsensky in Vienna (from 1819) and J. F. Schreiber in Esslingen (from 1831), and Denmark had
toy-theatre publishers of its own. Figures were moved from the side with rods or
in grooves, between the wing passages (Wikipedia, «Papiertheater»,
https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papiertheater). This cartridge follows the tradition's parts and
its slide from the side. No historical sheet is reproduced: every outline and the scene sets are
computed by the script.
