## What it is

Honeycomb tissue decorations fold flat as a thin book and open into a ball of cells. The trick is
in the glue: every tissue sheet is glued to the next along straight stripes, and each pair of
sheets uses the stripes the previous pair skipped. Pull the covers apart and the tissue between the
welds has to spread, so it opens into rows of cells, like honeycomb.

## Geometry

Every page has the same outline, with a straight **spine** along its bottom edge:

- **ball** and **fan**: a half disc whose diameter is the spine (`size` is the diameter);
- **bell**: a half bell whose axis is the spine (`size` is the bell's height): narrow at the top,
  flaring toward the mouth, with a small lip.

The glue stripes are `4 mm` wide and run perpendicular to the spine, from 3 mm above it to 3 mm
below the page edge, every `spacing` millimetres. Sheet `k` is welded to sheet `k + 1` on stripes
`j ≡ k (mod 2)`, the alternation that makes the cells. Each cell is two stripe spacings wide. A
card cover is welded to the outer face of the first and of the last tissue sheet.

## The mechanism

The document is physical (tier C). The sheets are laid in a stack 0.03 mm apart, inside tissue's
collision thickness, so every weld holds in the initial frame. Each weld is a `glue` join (62 at
the defaults). The two covers are kinematic boards: the bottom one stays put, and a `hinge` about
the spine, driven by the **Open** control, turns the top one up to 360° for the ball and the bell,
or 180° for the fan, a half ball that sits flat against a wall.

The cells exist only because tissue bends and stretches between the welds, so the opening needs the
physics engine. The studio preview cannot drive a hinge whose moving board is glued to paper: it
shows the closed stack and disables the Open control. Real decorations use many more sheets; the
default of 16 keeps the simulation light.

Flat pleated paper fans and rosettes are a different object: they are accordion-pleated, not
honeycomb.

## Making one by hand

1. Draw a guide on scrap paper: the page outline and the glue stripes, numbered alternately.
2. Lay a tissue page on the guide and run a glue stick along the odd stripes. Lay the next page
   exactly on top and glue along the even stripes. Repeat, alternating, until the stack is done.
3. Glue a card cover on each outside face and let the stack dry flat under a weight.
4. Open the covers around the spine until they meet (ball, bell), or halfway (fan), and fasten
   them with a clip or glue.

The PDF and SVG exports give the page and cover outlines at full size; cut a stack of tissue at once
with sharp scissors or a craft knife.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `shape` | ball (opens 360°), fan (half ball, opens 180°) or bell (opens 360°) |
| `size` | ball diameter or bell height along the spine, mm |
| `sheets` | number of tissue sheets in the stack |
| `spacing` | distance between neighbouring glue stripes, mm |
| `colour` | tissue colour |

## History and sources

Tissue honeycomb decorations are a common paper craft. Craft guides describe the method used here:
tissue pages stacked with glue on alternating lines, cut to a half circle, closed between card
covers and opened into a ball. This cartridge makes no claim about the craft's origin.

- Hobbycraft, "How to make tissue paper honeycombs":
  https://www.hobbycraft.co.uk/ideas/how-to-make-tissue-paper-honeycombs.html
