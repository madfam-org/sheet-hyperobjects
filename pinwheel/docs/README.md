## What it is

A pinwheel is a paper wheel on a stick that spins when you blow on it or run with it. The classic
one is made from a single square: cut along the diagonals from each corner towards the middle,
bring one corner of each of the four triangles over to the centre, and push a pin through the
corners, the centre and into a stick. This cartridge makes that one (`blades` = 4) and an
eight-bladed version from two squares pinned together, the upper one smaller and turned 45°, its
blades curled under the arches of the lower one.

## Geometry

The square's diagonals are cut from the corners and stop at `centre` × half the diagonal, so the
middle holds together. In each triangle the first corner (counter-clockwise) curls: three lines
perpendicular to its diagonal, at distances s, s + a and s + a + b from the centre, fold 70°, 110°
and 110° towards the viewer, which takes the blade up, over and back down. The corner lands
exactly on the centre when the two legs of the arch are equal and b = s + 2a·cos 70°; with the
curl starting just outside the cuts, a = (D − 2s) / (2 + 2·cos 70°), D being half the diagonal.
The centre has a 2 mm pin hole. The stick is a strip of 300 g/m² card, 10 mm wide and 1.4 × the
square long, with a kiss-cut cross where the pin goes in.

## The mechanism

- **Making it.** The fold program curls the blades one by one (four or eight steps). It is
  kinematic: each blade is a chain of rigid pieces.
- **Spinning.** The wheels are kinematic sheets turned by a `rotate` mechanism about the pin, the
  normal of the stick at the pin mark, on the `spin` control (0–360°).
- **Wind.** The `wind` control (m/s) is declared for the engine's air model (wave 3), which will
  turn the pinwheel from the airflow. The preview does not use it.

**Preview and engine.** The making and the spin play in the studio preview. Real blades are curled,
not creased: the preview folds three lines per blade, and the physics engine bends the paper
between them. Wind-driven spinning, with its speed set by the blades' pitch and the air, needs the
engine's air model (wave 3).

## Making one by hand

1. Export the sheets (`svg`, `pdf`, `dxf` or `hpgl`) on two-coloured paper if you like.
2. Cut the square and the four diagonal slits; punch the pin hole. The three curl lines of each
   blade show where it bends: curl it there without pressing a crease.
3. Bring the marked corner of each blade to the centre, one after the other, and hold them with
   the pin. Push the pin through the centre and into the stick at the cross, with a small bead or
   a scrap of card behind the wheel so it turns freely.
4. For eight blades, lay the smaller square on the larger one turned 45°, curl its blades under
   the large arches, and pin everything together.

## History and sources

The paper pinwheel is a folk toy with no single inventor. Outside North America it is often called
a windmill, and in the nineteenth-century United States any wind-driven toy held up by a running
child was called a whirligig. Toys of the same kind, made of palm or pandanus leaves, developed
independently in Polynesia (Te Rangi Hīroa, *Samoan Material Culture*, 1930). Sources: Wikipedia,
«Pinwheel (toy)», https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinwheel_(toy), and the works it cites. The
geometry here is computed by the cartridge.
