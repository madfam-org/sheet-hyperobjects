## What it is

A tunnel book is a stack of cut frames held apart by accordion (leporello) side walls. Seen through
its front window, the layers add up to one scene with depth. In this diorama the scene is a forest:

- `frames` frames of trees stand in front of a solid back scene;
- the first frame is the cover, with tall trees and a canopy hanging from the top;
- behind it the ground rises, the trees grow smaller and more numerous, and a clearing in the middle
  of every frame lines up into a path.

Pulling the cover opens the tunnel.

It is a composition of the [`leporello`](../../leporello/docs/README.md) and the layered cut frames
of the [`tunnel-book`](../../tunnel-book/docs/README.md), built differently from the latter. In
`tunnel-book` the frames are chained by straps and carry their own walls. Here the two walls are
continuous leporello strips, and each wall folds half of every frame out of itself. The kernel
builder is `pliego.book.pleated_tunnel`; no cartridge's script is copied.

## Geometry

Three sheets, all generated by `main.py`:

- **the back scene:** a solid panel with two side tabs folded forward, sky coloured;
- **two wall strips** (left and right, mirror images of each other).

Each wall strip runs from the back to the front. It starts with a 12 mm foot glued onto the back
scene's tab. Then, for each frame from the deepest to the cover:

- an accordion **V**: two panels of length `depth`, peak outward, valley-mountain-valley folds;
- a **box pleat**: half the frame (`frame_width`/2) going in to the middle, a 1 mm strip, and the
  same half coming back out to the wall.

Each frame is therefore two double half-frames, one from each wall, meeting at the middle seam. The
cover's two halves are tied together at the seam with two short ties (`string` joins).

The forest windows are original silhouettes:

- round-crowned trees, poplars, and firs with branching sides, on a gently rolling ground;
- `forest` selects mixed, firs only, or round crowns and poplars;
- the cover adds a hanging canopy of leaves.

Each frame has one window, drawn across the whole frame and crossing the seam once, in the
clearing. The builder cuts it through both layers of both half-frames, mirrored about each pleat's
strip so the layers line up. Tree tips are blunt so that every bridge stays wide. Both walls are
checked with `pliego.papercut.analyze`: one piece, every bridge at least 1.5 mm on 160 g/m² card or
2 mm on 250 g/m². A frame's border is 4 mm, or 5 mm on 250 g/m².

`main.py` fails loud unless:

- the walls' crease angles reproduce the analytic pose (`pose_error` below 1e-6 mm, shut, half open
  and open);
- the frames stand parallel, evenly spaced and aligned left and right.

## The fold program

Every crease of a wall is vertical, so each wall is a planar chain. Every crease angle is an affine
function of the walls' half-angle φ: the V peak turns 2φ, the folds where a wall meets a frame turn
90° − φ, the first fold at the foot turns φ, and the pleat folds stay at 90°. The opening is therefore one linear step, exact at every point of the
blend.

The initial frame is the shut book: φ = 86.8°, frames 3 mm apart. The kinematic program has one
step:

1. **Pull the cover: the forest opens.** φ goes to 30°, and the frames move to 2·`depth`·cos 30° +
   1 mm apart (32.2 mm by default).

The sequence control starts at the end, with the tunnel open.

**Preview and engine.** The studio preview plays the whole opening, because the walls carry the
frames in their own fold trees and the back scene and the walls' feet stay still. The glue at the
feet, the cover ties, and the card's springback against the hand need the physics engine.

## Making one by hand

1. Export the three sheets (`svg`, `dxf`, `pdf` or `hpgl`). Cuts are on the cut layer, every fold on
   the score layer. A wall strip is about 640 mm long at the defaults: cut it from a long strip of
   card, or from two pieces joined at a V's peak with a strip of tape behind.
2. Score every fold, then cut the windows.
3. Fold each half-frame's pleat: in, across the 1 mm strip, and out. A dab of glue inside the pleat
   keeps its two layers together.
4. Fold the walls' V's and glue each wall's foot onto a side tab of the back scene.
5. Close the tunnel and tie the two cover halves at the seam. To open, hold the back and pull the
   cover.

## Parameters

| id | meaning | default | range |
|---|---|---|---|
| `frames` | cut frames, with the cover | 4 | 3–6 |
| `frame_width` | width of a frame | 120 mm | 90–180 mm |
| `frame_height` | height of a frame | 90 mm | 70–140 mm |
| `depth` | length of an accordion panel | 18 mm | 12–30 mm |
| `stock` | `cardstock-160` or `cardstock-250` | `cardstock-160` | |
| `forest` | `mixed`, `conifers` or `grove` | `mixed` | |

Presets: `three-frames`, `six-frames` (14 mm panels) and `wide` (160 × 100 mm frames of firs).

## History and sources

Paper peepshows with accordion sides and layered cut-out panels date from the eighteenth century.
The Victoria and Albert Museum holds «The Tunnel», a paper peepshow of about 1825 with five cut-out
panels and a peep-hole in a concertina, whose expanded bellows create the impression of depth
(https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1418244/the-tunnel-paper-peepshow). The Smithsonian Libraries
document earlier peepshow work by Martin Engelbrecht (https://library.si.edu/es/tags/martin-engelbrecht).
Tunnel books remain a book-arts and classroom staple (Topeka & Shawnee County Public Library,
«Tunnel books: an easy step by step», https://tscpl.org/articles/tunnel-books-an-easy-step-by-step).

The box-pleat walls that fold the frames out of themselves, and the trees, are original to this
cartridge. No published tunnel book is reproduced.
