# Mountain fold

| | Title | Summary |
|---|---|---|
| en | Mountain fold | The same square folded the other way: the halves turn toward the back, the crease rises like a ridge, and the fold angle is negative. |
| es | Pliegue en montaña | El mismo cuadrado doblado al revés: las mitades giran hacia el reverso, el pliegue se levanta como una cresta y su ángulo es negativo. |
| fr | Pli montagne | Le même carré plié dans l'autre sens : les moitiés tournent vers le verso, le pli se dresse comme une crête et son angle est négatif. |
| pt | Dobra em montanha | O mesmo quadrado dobrado ao contrário: as metades giram em direção ao verso, o vinco se ergue como uma crista e seu ângulo é negativo. |

A mountain fold is a valley fold made from the other side. Fold the paper away from you, behind
itself, and the crease stands up from the sheet like the ridge of a mountain. Turn the folded
square over and you are holding a valley fold. The two are one fold seen from two sides, and the
difference only matters because paper has a front and a back.

## Geometry and sign convention

Same square as `valley-fold`: side `size` (150 mm by default), lower-left corner at the origin,
y up, with the crease along the **diagonal** `(0, 0)–(size, size)` or as a **book fold**
`(size/2, 0)–(size/2, size)`.

The crease carries assignment `M` and a **negative** fold angle. In the FOLD 1.2 convention used
by the sheet document (spec v1 §1), the fold angle is the deviation from flat in degrees: positive
for a valley (faces rotate toward the front), negative for a mountain (faces rotate toward the
back). The `angle` parameter is a magnitude (30–180°). The kernel's `Sheet.mountain()` writes it
as `−angle`, and the script refuses to build if the sign comes out wrong. So a square folded flat
carries `−180`, and the `target:folded` frame lifts the moving half to −Y (behind the sheet), where
the valley fold's frame lifts it to +Y.

| | Valley fold | Mountain fold |
|---|---|---|
| assignment | `V` | `M` |
| fold angle | `+180` | `−180` |
| faces rotate toward | the front (+Y) | the back (−Y) |
| with kami, the outside of the fold is | white (the back) | red (the front) |
| diagram line (Yoshizawa–Randlett) | dashes | dashes and dots |

**Front and back colours.** Kami is red on the front and white on the back (the stock card's
appearance block). A mountain fold closes the two white back faces onto each other, so the red
front stays outside. Choosing between the two decides which colour a finished model shows.

## What the engine is asked to simulate

The behaviour described here is the engine contract of the sheet-document spec v1 (§4, §5): the
cartridge writes the document, and the engine is specified to compute the motion from it.

One physical step, *Mountain-fold along the diagonal*, drives the hinge to `−angle` with the
crease stiffness raised by the hand factor. The flaps are physical paper with the stock's bending
stiffness and weight, and the crease takes a plastic set once it passes the stock's yield angle
(35° for kami, a documented estimate on the stock card). `target:folded` is the kernel's exact
face-tree pose of the end state; every edge keeps its length in it.

Foil paper (`foil-paper`) has a yield angle of only 2° on its stock card, an estimate for paper
backed with aluminium. It keeps almost any fold you give it, which is why it is chosen for
sculpted models. Compare it with kami at `angle = 90`.

## Making one

1. Cut a square (export `svg`, `dxf`, `pdf` or `hpgl`; the mountain line is drawn in the
   mountain style of the exporter's palette).
2. Coloured side up, fold the square *behind* along the line: either turn it over and make a
   valley fold, or pinch the line from below and push the flaps away from you.
3. Press the ridge flat.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Meaning | Default |
|---|---|---|
| `size` | side of the square (mm) | 150 |
| `stock` | `kami`, `foil-paper`, `copy-80`, `tant` | `kami` |
| `fold_line` | `diagonal` (corner to corner) or `book` (edge to edge) | `diagonal` |
| `angle` | fold angle magnitude (°); written as negative, 180 is flat | 180 |

Presets: `book-fold`, `half-open` (90°).

## History and sources

A traditional technique. The diagramming convention that names and draws it is the
Yoshizawa–Randlett system, introduced by Akira Yoshizawa in 1954 and later adopted and extended in
English-language books by Samuel Randlett and Robert Harbin (Wikipedia, «Yoshizawa–Randlett
system», https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshizawa%E2%80%93Randlett_system). No diagram is
reproduced; the geometry is generated by this cartridge's `main.py`.
