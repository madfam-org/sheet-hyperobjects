## What it is

The twist fold generalises from the square to any regular polygon. The regular polygons that tile
the plane on their own are the triangle, the square and the hexagon. This cartridge carries the
two besides the square: the **hexagon twist** (six pleats, on a
regular hexagonal sheet) and the **triangle twist** (three pleats, on an equilateral triangle).
Both are generated by the kernel's `pliego.tessellate.twist(n, …)` with n = 6 and n = 3. Hexagon and
triangle twists are the cells of tessellations on the triangular and hexagonal grids. The square
case has its own cartridge, `square-twist`.

| Mode | Sheet | Centre | Pleats |
|---|---|---|---|
| `hexagon` | regular hexagon | regular hexagon, side `inner` | 6 pairs |
| `triangle` | equilateral triangle | equilateral triangle, side `inner` | 3 pairs |

## Geometry

At each corner of the central n-gon the creases sit at 0° and 180° − 360°/n (the polygon's two
edges) and at 270° − 360°/n + α and 270° + α (the pleat lines), measured from one edge. The
sectors are therefore

    180° − 360°/n,  90° + α,  360°/n,  90° − α

| n | sectors |
|---|---|
| 6 | 120°, 90° + α, 60°, 90° − α |
| 3 | 60°, 90° + α, 120°, 90° − α |

The alternate sums are 180°, so **Kawasaki's theorem** holds for any twist angle `α`. Three
valleys and one mountain satisfy **Maekawa's theorem**.

The **big-little-big rule** decides which twist angles work. A sector that is strictly smaller
than both its neighbours must be bounded by one mountain and one valley. In the triangle twist,
the 60° sector lies inside the centre triangle, between two valleys. It must therefore never be
the strictly smallest sector, which means 90° − α < 60°, or **α > 30°**. The triangle's slider
starts at 32°. For the hexagon every angle in range passes. The script runs
`pliego.flatfold.check` on every vertex and fails loud if a test fails. The kernel itself refuses
pleats that would cross inside the sheet.

The hexagon's crease directions have irrational slopes. Kawasaki is therefore confirmed in floating
point, within the kernel's grid-derived tolerance, rather than by the exact rational test; the
script prints how many vertices passed exactly.

**Rigid folding.** The script drives one centre valley 10° away from flat with the kernel's
motion solver (`pliego.kinematics.solve`). For both twists the solver finds no rigid motion
(`KinematicsError`), and the script prints:

    kinematics: rigid motion from flat driving a centre valley: none -> program is physical

## The fold program

The program is `physical`, with three steps:

1. **Pre-fold the pleats halfway.** Pleats go to ±90°, the centre edges to 20°.
2. **Turn the centre polygon.** Pleats go to ±150°, the centre edges to 120°.
3. **Press the twist flat.** Every crease goes to ±180°.

The document carries a `target:flat-folded` frame with the exact flat-folded state.

**What the preview shows and what needs the physics engine.** The studio preview poses the faces
along a spanning tree of the crease pattern. The last step, the flat-folded twist, is exact: every
crease is at 180° and Kawasaki holds at every vertex, so the faces meet. In the two intermediate
steps no rigid state exists. The face tree then opens seams between faces it does not join, where
real paper bends. The physics engine (tier C) supplies that bending.

## Making one by hand

1. Plot or print the pattern (SVG, PDF, DXF or HPGL) on a hexagonal or triangular sheet, and
   score every line.
2. Precrease each line in its own direction: fold it, press it, open it again.
3. Pre-fold the pleats, push the centre polygon down from the front and turn it. The pleats then
   wrap round it in a pinwheel.
4. Press it flat.

In the hexagon twist all six pleats have to move together, so collapse it gradually and keep
turning the centre as the pleats close.

## Parameters

| Parameter | Modes | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `alpha_hex` | `hexagon` | twist angle α, degrees (5–40) |
| `alpha_tri` | `triangle` | twist angle α, degrees (32–60; must exceed 30°) |
| `inner` | both | side of the centre polygon, mm |
| `sheet_size` | both | twice the distance from the sheet's centre to its corners, mm |
| `stock` | both | kami, copy paper, kozo washi or tant |

Presets: `gentle` (α = 8° for the hexagon, 33° for the triangle) and `steep` (35° and 55°, centre
side 20 mm).

## History and sources

Twist folds on hexagons and triangles belong to the same family as the square twist. Shuzo
Fujimoto explored them in the 1970s; they are now studied as flat-foldable crease patterns with
a general theory.

- S. Fujimoto, *Sōzōsei o kaihatsu suru rittai origami* ("Creative invention by 3-D origami"),
  self-published, 1976.
- R. J. Lang, *Twists, Tilings, and Tessellations: Mathematical Methods for Geometric Origami*,
  CRC Press, 2018. Twist folds on regular polygons, and the conditions on their angles.
- T. A. Evans, R. J. Lang, S. P. Magleby and L. L. Howell, "Rigidly foldable origami twists", in
  *Origami⁶* (American Mathematical Society, 2015).
- T. C. Hull, *Origametry: Mathematical Methods in Paper Folding*, Cambridge University Press,
  2020. Kawasaki, Maekawa and the big-little-big lemma.
